I have initially realised Polymarket bets carry some resemblance to options in January and made short script which took copy-pasted by hand table from nasdaq website and Polymarket. It identified some mispricings but small since it relied on one time copy-paste. 
In September, using Claude Code, I took a more structured approach, scraping both platforms every 15 minutes during open sessions to collect a large amount of data and find mispricings. 

The essence of this project is that some Polymarket bets carry a resemblance to options traded on US stock exchanges. I’ve learnt from a YouTube video that the Black-Scholes model is a standard way to price options nowadays, and that a little bit of math allows us to derive a probability that a stock touches or closes above a given price before a given date from publicly available options data.

This model assumes that if we had a perfect 1-to-1 comparison of a Polymarket bet to a Nasdaq/NYSE option, the latter we expect to be much closer to the truth, since the volume is significantly higher and mainly dictated by professionals in the industry, whereas for Polymarket, I suppose, it is mostly internet gamblers. 

The comparison is, however, not perfect and needs some amount of interpolation (e.g., there may not be options at the exact price a bet is listed and expiry date differs, or that formula is for European-type options, Polymarket lists essentially American). The amount of approximation goes beyond what I originally assumed, and I believe that at the current stage, some of this interpolation is done suboptimally. 
Despite that, (which can be seen at least from a careful consideration of commit history) the model still showed promise. 
For example, in a recent test, the model, as it was committed on 1 October 2026, averaged a 10.7% weekly return over the following six weeks of weekly hit markets (with big swings from −8% to +32%) while random betting lost 18.8% in total. I realise that given the huge correlation between the bets within a week, 6 weeks is a rather small sample, and the finding may not be statistically significant yet. 
However, initially the performance was reviewed as a form of backtest. It took a pre-implemented model and ran it against scraped data. Since it evaluated a model written before data collection for a given period, it was a proof of concept to me and convinced me the idea works. The initial strength of this approach was that it allowed us to evaluate efficiency as if we had real-time option data, whereas publicly available API delays it by 15 minutes. But I realised it has little significance to an outsider, as this way of reporting performance can be easily falsified. Since 6th November, I began reporting the bets the basic model places on one type of market in real time, so that the model’s efficiency can be seen from the push history. It is also important because, while I believe the quality of the code here is rather low and many things need improvement, it still works to an extent. 

The idea for a basic model is:
Look at bets and options, use the engine to calculate mispricings (that include fees). Take all bets that are mispriced beyond a certain threshold and wait until the bet resolves. Notably, since the volume of these markets is very limited, calculate the maximum size of the bet such that the mispricing is still larger than the threshold. 

We have to wait until the bet resolves since these markets are small and the spread between the buy and sell price virtually erases all the profit. The spread, however, doesn’t apply if we don’t sell the bet but rather wait till it is settled. 

Since most stocks are largely correlated, there is a risk that despite positive expected value, all bets will lose. 
I have an idea for a model that hedges this risk by balancing the type of bets it takes (basically balancing the amount of bets that predict the stock will go down and up). Another good idea would be to construct a few models, perhaps with different methods from what we have, to approximate and take the bet only if all or the majority of models agree it pays off.

All of the code was written by AI (Claude Code). My part was the ideas and the decisions - which markets to target, how to price them, which models to test, and how to evaluate them. Along the way, I revised many of my initial assumptions, for example, moving from “close above” to “hit” markets once their volume collapsed, and holding every bet to resolution once the spread turned out to erase the profit of selling early. I paid much less attention to code structure, which led to a repository that is difficult to scale and contains hidden approximations that are likely imperfect in places.

I consider the current phase a sufficient proof of concept: despite the approximations, the results so far suggest the idea works on real data. What the project needs now is a sounder design, so in December, after my first-semester finals, I plan to take a careful approach to this project, evaluating each step in the decision pipeline and arriving first at an MVP - only a basic model for weekly HIT markets- before designing more advanced models and expanding to other option-resembling market types.
